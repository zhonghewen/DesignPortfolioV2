The Problem

Migrating a complex legacy help system to an AI assistant — without breaking seller trust.

Amazon Seller Central was replacing its traditional help page with an AI assistant, accessible from every page across the platform. This was not a greenfield product — it had to absorb the full complexity of an existing system that thousands of sellers relied on daily.

The migration created two core challenges:

  • Adoption & metrics protection — Ensuring sellers would embrace the new format without harming key business metrics like satisfaction scores and contact rates
  • Scalability — Designing a UX pattern that could handle the full breadth of workflows in the legacy system

Before — Traditional Help Page

After — Seller AI Assistant

Through usability testing and early launch data, we discovered that verbose AI responses were causing high abandonment rates — validating the need to redesign the core interaction pattern for clarity and efficiency before scaling further.

02 / 06

Challenge 1 — Space Constraints

Compressing a full help surface into a conversational panel.

The AI assistant lives in a small side panel — far less real estate than the traditional help page it was replacing. This forced a fundamental rethinking of how to present information, actions, and workflows within a constrained space.

Legacy help content included rich tables, multi-column layouts, and step-by-step instructional content — none of which could simply be ported into a chat interface without becoming unusable.

The Hypothesis: If we systematically mapped every help feature category onto the assistant panel, we could identify which patterns compressed cleanly and which required entirely new interaction approaches.

Design Approach

I collaborated with stakeholders to prototype and map ten key feature categories from the old help page onto the AI assistant panel — covering everything from policy lookup to case creation to escalation flows.

For each category, I evaluated three dimensions:

01.

Information density — how much content needs to be visible at once, and what could be collapsed, progressive-disclosed, or removed without losing utility

02.

Interaction complexity — whether the feature required forms, selections, or confirmations, and how to nest those into a linear chat thread

03.

Trust signals — what information sellers needed to see to feel confident the assistant understood their issue before taking any action

Design Output

The mapping exercise produced a feature-to-pattern taxonomy — a shared reference that aligned product, engineering, and content teams on what was achievable in Phase 1 versus what needed phased delivery.

03 / 06

Challenge 2 — Multi-step Workflows

Enabling complex self-service tasks inside a linear chat thread.

Many of Seller Central's self-service tools involve sequential steps — submitting evidence, selecting options, confirming actions, and tracking outcomes — all within a single support interaction. The traditional help page handled this through dedicated full-page flows.

Inside a chat panel, the entire flow had to happen inline, without navigating away — and had to remain clear to sellers at every step.

The Hypothesis: An inline interaction pattern — where UI components render directly within the chat thread — would let sellers complete multi-step workflows without breaking the conversational context or losing their place.

Design Approach: The Inline Interaction Pattern

I designed a system where the AI assistant could embed structured UI components — forms, selections, confirmations — directly into the chat thread. Each component appeared in context, tied to a specific step in the workflow, rather than as a separate page or modal.

This preserved the conversational flow while giving sellers the precision and control they needed to complete high-stakes tasks like case creation and escalation.

Key design decisions in the pattern:

  • Progressive commitment — each step is confirmed before advancing, preventing accidental submissions
  • Inline error recovery — validation and corrections happen within the same thread, not on a separate error page
  • Persistent context — previously captured information (ASINs, case IDs) remains visible throughout the flow, reducing re-entry burden

The Validation Signal

Early Launch Data Confirmed the Inline Bet

What We Found

Early testing revealed that abandonment rates doubled when the assistant used verbose conversational responses instead of structured inline components — validating that sellers needed structured interaction, not narrative output, for task completion.

What It Changed

This data shifted the team's assumptions about conversational AI. The assistant's job was not to explain — it was to compress a complex workflow into the smallest possible interaction cost. Conversation was the wrapper, not the mechanism.

Challenge 3 — Scalability

Building a component system that could absorb an entire legacy platform.

Designing a good inline pattern for one workflow wasn't enough — the assistant needed to handle hundreds of distinct use cases across Seller Central. Designing custom interactions for each case at that scale was not feasible.

The Hypothesis: If the dominant interaction patterns across all legacy workflows could be extracted and systematized, a small component library could cover the full surface — making future feature migration a matter of assembly, not bespoke design.

Design Approach: Pattern Extraction

Working with subject-matter experts across teams, I analyzed the highest-volume seller workflows to identify which interaction patterns appeared most frequently. Across hundreds of use cases, the dominant types reduced to a manageable set:

01.

Input fields — free-text or structured entry for identifiers, descriptions, and context

02.

Radio buttons & dropdowns — single-selection from a known option set

03.

Modal selections — multi-step confirmation dialogs for high-stakes or irreversible actions

04.

Status displays — read-only inline summaries of system state (case progress, resolution status)

These patterns became the foundation of a component library — a shared design and engineering resource that could be composed to represent any legacy workflow without requiring net-new design work.

The Trade-off

Ideal Vision vs. Phase 1 Reality

The Ideal

The long-term vision required breaking down all workflows into atomic, composable components — each action a discrete unit that the assistant could chain in any order. This would have produced the most elegant experience, fully native to the conversational format.

The Constraint

Atomic decomposition requires significant engineering time — rebuilding each legacy workflow from scratch rather than migrating existing logic. This was not feasible within the Phase 1 timeline.

The Decision

In Phase 1, complex workflows launch via additional side panels rather than fully inline flows — less elegant, but it meets the core migration requirement and preserves the seller experience without delays. Inline decomposition rolls out progressively in later phases.

The full impact on seller adoption and task completion is currently being measured as the migration scales across more workflows.

Reflections

What a large-scale AI migration taught me about pragmatic design.

  • 01. Phased Delivery Is a Design Decision Large-scale migrations require accepting a gap between the ideal vision and Phase 1 reality. Designing that gap deliberately — with a clear rationale and a path forward — is more valuable than holding out for perfection. The component library gave the team a shared blueprint so phased delivery felt intentional, not compromised.
  • 02. Data Beats Assumptions About Conversational AI Going in, the assumption was that a more conversational, explanatory AI would feel more natural. Early launch data said the opposite — structure outperformed narrative for task-oriented sellers. The willingness to collect early signal and redesign around it, rather than defend the original direction, was what made the difference.
  • 03. Systems Thinking Scales Better Than Features Designing individual screens for individual workflows would have been fast in the short term and impossible to maintain at scale. Investing the time to extract patterns and build a shared component library created a durable foundation — one that made future feature migration a matter of assembly, not repeated bespoke design work.
