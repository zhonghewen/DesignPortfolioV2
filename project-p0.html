The Problem

Low-signal inputs were breaking the AI pipeline.

Amazon Sellers often face complex issues. When they seek support, they frequently provide vague or incomplete information, resulting in input quality too low for automated systems to resolve.

The gap between what sellers naturally provide and what AI models actually need creates a bottleneck across the entire support funnel.

Low-Signal · What We Received Noise

Keywords with no actionable intent.

Sellers typing fragments into open text fields. The model can't classify, so the case escalates to a human.

Open text field — input "human"
Open text field — input "111"
Open text field — input "FBA"
High-Signal · What the Model Needs Signal

A complete, structured problem description.

Issue description + specific identifiers or supporting documentation + a desired outcome. Model-ready on the first turn.

Structured intent

Update product title of ASIN B001234567 to "Laptop Backpack, for 16" laptop, lightweight, black".

The Goal: Redesign the support funnel end-to-end — guiding sellers to provide the right signal, confirming their intent, and making automated execution feel safe enough to trust — without adding friction at any step.

02 / 08

The Opportunity

Treating the support journey as a conversion funnel.

I analyzed the end-to-end support journey as a conversion funnel. The goal was to maximize automated resolution by improving data quality at two critical stages —

  1. The Intake Stage
  2. The Clarification Stage

And

  1. Increasing seller adoption of automated execution.

Experiment A — Effortless Intake

The Problem: The Burden of Precision

Sellers struggled to articulate technical problems in writing. They would provide "low-signal" descriptions that failed to trigger backend models, leading to frustration and immediate human escalation.

Design Approach: Root Cause Investigation

I conducted a two-part investigation to identify why sellers struggled to describe their issues effectively:

01.

3-Year Research Synthesis — Identified psychological barriers including fear of inaccuracy and identification anxiety. Discovered a dominant "Minimum Effort" behavior where busy sellers avoided complex typing.

"Whenever we mention our issue in the case, sometimes we do not get the correct words or get the right idea… And as a result, we are sent to the wrong team that can't fix our issue."

— Amazon Sellers

"Which is to be honest, I like being lazy, and I just ask customer support to do all that for me, and I just say it. Fast and saving sellers' time are important."

— Amazon Sellers
02.

1,000-Entry Production Audit — Revealed that critical "signals" were already present in artifacts (emails, screenshots, reports) sellers possessed but lacked a low-friction way to share.

Audit example:

"The following shipment in Removal Order: **** was removed to our warehouse but is lost outbound. This shipped over 30 days ago but was not received as shown in the tracking below.

Carrier: AMZL   TrackingNumber: ****   TrackingUrl:

Date of Shipment: ****

| FNSKU | Unit Quantity | Price Per Unit | Total Amount |
| **** | **** | **** | **** |
| **** | **** | **** | **** |

Please reimburse, thank you!"

The Hypothesis: By capturing "ground truth" data directly from existing artifacts would bypass typing friction and anxiety.

The Solution: From "Write" to "Share"

I redesigned the intake entry point to let sellers show the problem rather than describe it — by attaching screenshots, error logs, shipment reports, or certifications directly to their case.

The system then uses these attachments to pre-fill the issue description, reducing the burden of having to articulate a technical problem from scratch.

Before

After

The Pushback

Turning a rejected concept into a roadmap item.

Resistance

Engineering initially rejected the attachment concept — citing infrastructure costs and immature image-analysis tools in mid 2025.

Action

Rather than escalating or compromising, I ran a smoke test using internal tools to demonstrate that existing Claude 3.5 Sonnet models could already parse attachment metadata and pre-fill description fields.

Result

The concept shipped in phases — Phase 1 allows sellers to attach screenshots and bypass the description field entirely, with pre-filling planned for later phases. It went from rejected to a roadmap item.

Outcome

Experiment A results — Phase 1 shipped and delivered measurable results across the seller base.

Experiment B — Rephrase Issues

The Problem: The Verification Gap

Even when sellers attempted to describe their issue, they often missed key information — and knew it.

  • The user hurdle: Anxiety that incomplete input leads to an incorrect resolution
  • The operational bottleneck: Without complete, structured information, the model's resolution confidence remained low — causing unnecessary human escalations

The Hypothesis: If the system could rephrase the seller's input into a clear, structured statement and surface missing information in context, sellers would feel genuinely understood — and the model would get the signal quality it needed to resolve automatically.

Design Approach: Defining a "Good Rephrase"

The core design challenge wasn't the interaction pattern — it was defining what a good rephrase actually looked like.

I selected the top 5 issue types (covering 21% of contact volume) and wrote ideal rephrases against real production seller queries, establishing UX quality principles that worked for both sellers and the model.

From there I drove a 5-iteration loop with the science team — documenting where each rephrase fell short and translating failure patterns into actionable feedback. Other stakeholders joined selectively; I was the constant from start to finish.

The Challenge

The Tech Approach Didn't Meet the UX Quality Bar

Initial Approach

Engineering proposed a static template approach to avoid a lengthy security review.

Trust Break

Testing revealed it surfaced irrelevant prompts that had nothing to do with the seller's issue — breaking trust immediately.

The Push

I partnered with the Applied Science team to push for a dynamic, case-based generation approach instead. It required going through security review, but it was the only path to quality sellers would actually trust.

The Solution: Guided Structural Scaffolding

Once the system generates a rephrase, it presents the seller's intent back to them as a clear, structured statement — with any missing critical information surfaced inline as specific fields to complete. With this approach, sellers see exactly what the system understood and exactly what it still needs.

After

Validation

Unmoderated usability testing with 6 sellers showed 5 out of 6 rating satisfaction at 6 out of 7. The quality bar we set was validated in an unexpected way — sellers assumed the rephrase was a scripted demo, not a live system responding to their real input:

"I felt that (capturing provided ID and display them in tag) was good because sometimes you put in an ASIN or a SKU and you feel like it just kinda get lost or it doesn't get picked up."

Experiment C — Automated Execution

Automating path selection to eliminate wrong-path escalations.

The Problem: Wrong Path, Lost Trust

When navigating self-service, sellers are often presented with ambiguous sub-issue options that look nearly identical — making it difficult to choose the right path. Take listing issues as an example: sellers are asked to distinguish between options like

"received an error code or message," "product identifier related issues," and "product identifier exemptions"

— options that look nearly identical.

"Product identifier? What's that? Um, it's not clear really because product identifier is the ASIN or the name of it? Dunno."

— Amazon Seller

The result: sellers choose the wrong path or abandon self-service entirely, driving avoidable human escalations.

The Hypothesis: By automating the solution selection entirely, sellers receive a more accurate resolution without ever having to navigate confusing options.

Design Approach: Designing for Latency

Automating the fix introduced a new challenge — the process takes 10+ seconds, a range where user attention drops sharply and abandonment spikes. Before designing the interaction, I conducted a literature review to establish a latency framework:

RangeUser Experience
Below 1sFeels nearly instantaneous
1–3sAcceptable, delay noticeable
4–10sTests user patience
10s+Upper limit of focused attention — high abandonment risk

Estimated latency data put our automated workflows at 9+ seconds on average. So the design challenge became: how do we keep sellers engaged and trusting a process they can't see?

The Challenge

Building Trust Without Streaming

The Constraint

The ideal solution — a streaming thinking model showing live progress — was ruled out due to compliance requirements. Instead, I had to design static status messages that felt transparent enough to maintain trust.

The Push

I advocated with the tech team to surface key variables inline — such as the extracted identifier — so sellers could see the system had correctly understood their issue, even without live updates.

The Solution: Key Status Display

I designed and tested two approaches to communicate progress during automated execution:

  • Option 1: Full status breakdown — all sub-steps remain visible after completion
  • Option 2: Key status only — sub-steps disappear after each stage, leaving only the key milestone

Option 1

Option 2

Validation

Unmoderated usability testing with 8 sellers validated the approach:

  • 5 of 8 sellers preferred Option 2 for its simplicity
  • Surfacing the seller's own identifiers inline was the strongest trust signal

"I like Option 2 better because there's less verbiage to read."

— Amazon Seller

"It understood what the situation was and gave me an explanation linked back to the situation."

— Amazon Seller

Critically, 0 of 8 sellers would abandon the flow at 10 seconds if the automation consistently delivered the right resolution — validating that sellers tolerate latency when they trust the outcome.

We proceeded with Option 2 as the final design.

Reflections

What I learned designing at the human-AI boundary.

  • 01. Designing for Minimum Effort The real design constraint wasn't technical — it was behavioral. The highest-leverage design decisions reduced effort to near zero, meeting sellers exactly where their behavior already was rather than asking them to change.
  • 02. Prototype Fast, Challenge Assumptions AI capabilities evolve faster than assumptions about what's possible. A quick proof of concept can often resolve a technical debate faster than any meeting. When attachment feasibility was questioned, a rapid AI-assisted demo settled it — turning a blocker into a roadmap item. In the AI era, "we can't do that" has a much shorter shelf life than it used to.
